\begin{abstract}
Mann showed in 2019 that the eye can be used as a camera: a square flickers at one position after another, the subject follows it, and the steady-state visual evoked potential (SSVEP) says how bright each position was. We move the method onto hardware a community health worker could carry: a Muse 2 headband with one cup electrode added over the visual cortex, and a Meta Quest 3S running the stimulus in its browser. We read a legible word in 18 minutes ($r = 0.91$ against time-shifted nulls of 0.23), eight colours as three calibrated colour planes (24 of 32 cells, reproduced over three sittings), and an acuity optotype drawn at random and sealed before the scan, named correctly from the EEG alone. The same recording gives an objective eye-strain reading: blinks stop while a cell flickers and burst when it goes dark, and the blink rate doubled between two sittings of one evening. We frame the result as a literacy-free visual screener for mobile health, with a prompt to rest the eyes as its first intervention. All results are from one subject.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

A camera needs a lens, a sensor and a readout. The eye has the first two; Mann's ``eye as a camera'' \cite{mann2019eye, mann2019wearsys} supplied the third. A square flickers at a fixed rate, the subject looks at it, and the visual cortex answers with an SSVEP whose strength follows the light the square puts into the fovea \cite{norcia2015ssvep, vialatte2010ssvep}. Scan the square across an image and the sequence of strengths is the image. The 2019 paper read ``NO CAMERAS'' and a portrait with a laboratory amplifier and 85 minutes of scanning.

This paper asks what the method can do on hardware that costs less than a phone and needs no technician: a Muse 2 headband (about \$250) with one cup electrode through its auxiliary input, a Meta Quest 3S (about \$300) running the stimulus as a web page, and a laptop. Our interest comes from the mobile health tradition of low-cost sensing in the hands of community health workers \cite{fletcher2010wearable, fletcher2017child, anand2018pulmonary}. A visual screen read from the EEG asks the subject to name no letter and press no button; they look at a dot. The same recording carries what the mobile health literature has long wanted from wearables, an objective reading of the subject's state \cite{fletcher2010icalm, fletcher2014mental}, here the eye strain that flicker produces.

We contribute: the first SSVEP imaging on a consumer VR headset with a consumer headband; colour by three calibrated planes; a sealed, objective acuity read-out; eye strain from the same recording; and the field engineering that made the method survive outside a laboratory, with a synthetic subject as regression test.

\section{Background}

The SSVEP is largest over the occipital midline (Oz) and strongest for stimuli in the central field \cite{norcia2015ssvep}, which is what makes a single flickering patch under the gaze one pixel per position \cite{mann2019eye}. SSVEP interfaces in head-mounted displays are established \cite{armengol2018sublime}; imaging on one, and on the Quest family, is not.

\textbf{Metaveillography of the eye.} The eye-as-camera idea grew out of a longer programme on seeing sight itself. Mann photographed a camera's capacity to see with a light that brightens when the camera sees it, the sightfield, a time-reversed lightfield captured by long exposure \cite{mann2014sightfield}, and named the sensing of sensors and of their capacity to sense metaveillance \cite{mann2016metaveillance}; the photographs are metaveillographs, the measurements metaveillogrammetry \cite{mann2021drones}. Janzen and Mann turned the method on the human eye, measuring how far its sensing extends through space as a biological veillance flux \cite{janzen2014veillance, janzen2015veillametrics}, and time-integrating that flux on objects as a veillogram \cite{janzen2018painting}. The 2019 paper let the brain report instead of the subject: an LED carried through the visual field by a plotter, its brightness set by the SSVEP from a Muse headband, photographed over a long exposure into a metaveillograph of the eye's own receptive field \cite{mann2019eye, mann2019wearsys}. The same group then mapped the visual field into a ``vidmap'' \cite{garcia2020painting} and read acuity from the SSVEP \cite{garcia2021acuity}. Two findings there carry this paper's health claims: the veillance field narrows when the observer ignores the flicker and widens when they track it, so it measures attention and not only optics, proposed in 2019 as a biomarker for attention problems in ADHD and ageing \cite{mann2019eye}; and the read-out is evidence rather than testimony, since nobody has to say what they see. Chromatic VEPs differ by colour axis and temporal frequency \cite{rabin1994chromatic}, which is why our colour planes are calibrated separately. The Muse headband is validated for event-related potentials \cite{krigolson2017muse} but has no electrode near Oz; the added cup is what makes imaging possible (cup alone $r = 0.92$, ear electrodes alone 0.36). A VR headset over the Muse improves the contact rather than degrading it \cite{mann2026float}.

Fletcher's group has built a decade of diagnostics around sensors a phone already has, for workers who are not specialists: pulmonary screening from cough and lung sounds \cite{chamberlain2016asthma, infante2017cough, anand2018pulmonary}, child health tools \cite{fletcher2017child}, printed diagnostics read by the camera \cite{fletcher2016printed}, thermal imaging with deep learning \cite{fletcher2021thermal, byun2021thermal}, blood pressure from photoplethysmography \cite{elgendi2019ppg}, and hand-vein patient identification \cite{fletcher2014vein}. Vision screening is a gap in that list: the Snellen chart and the Ishihara plates need a literate, cooperative subject who reports what they see. \rf{where vision screening sits in a community health worker's day} The second thread is the wearable as a window on state, from iCalm \cite{fletcher2010icalm} through sensor-plus-app systems for cognitive behavioural therapy \cite{fletcher2011cbt, boyer2012iheal, leonard2018homeless} to the just-in-time adaptive intervention \cite{nahum2018jitai}. \rf{JITAI framing}

\section{System}

\textbf{Hardware.} Muse 2 at 256\,Hz over Bluetooth to a phone, forwarded to the laptop over UDP; a gold cup with paste at Oz on the Muse auxiliary input, held by the headset strap; a Quest 3S; a laptop (Fig.~\ref{fig:setup}). Under \$600, nothing modified. The phone runs MuseLog \cite{vicol2026muselog}, the lab's own acquisition app for Android and iOS: it pairs with one or several Muse headbands, records the raw EEG with the auxiliary channel at 256\,Hz, the band powers and, on the Muse S Athena, the fNIRS channels, takes markers, and streams all of it live over OSC on UDP to any computer on the same network. A full-rate raw stream was added for this work: the app's decimated stream folded 34 to 49\,Hz onto the SSVEP band. \todo{MuseLog paper citation}

\textbf{Stimulus.} A web page served by the laptop and opened in the headset's browser. One square flickers between black and white, or black and a colour, at the target position; a dot marks it and the subject follows the dot. The page flips on the display's frame clock, so at 72\,fps the 12\,Hz flicker is exactly three frames on and three off, and 9\,Hz four and four; it reports the delivered frequency and its visibility, since a background tab renders at one frame per second.

\textbf{Session.} An eyes-closed alpha check confirms the cup is on the scalp. Calibration is six blocks of 8\,s full-field flicker on and off; a line statistic at the flicker frequency with an exact permutation test, and a $\dprime$ per electrode, decide whether to proceed and how to weight the electrodes. The scan visits every grid position in serpentine order, 8\,s each, with rest breaks on the subject's trigger, then revisits the positions whose black-or-white decision is uncertain. Each visit's pixel is the paper's whole-dwell ratio, power at the flicker frequency and its harmonic over the 14 to 50\,Hz floor, weighted across electrodes; visits are averaged and the picture thresholded by Otsu's method in the log domain \cite{otsu1979}. Every picture is scored against the target by correlation and against nulls in which the EEG is shifted in time by tens of seconds.

\textbf{Colour by planes.} Red, green and blue are three scans, each black-versus-colour with its own calibration; the three threshold decisions per cell give one of eight colours. Blue is weak (about 0.4 of red in power at the same contrast), so it gets a frequency sweep before the scan (6, 7.2, 9, 12\,Hz; 9\,Hz won on every sitting), a patch two cells wide, and a second visit to its ten least certain cells. Green gets a second pass; red, which has never missed a cell, one.

\textbf{Guards.} Five failures each cost a session before they were caught, from a laptop entering standby on battery to an ear electrode loose from minute 11; the driver now guards against four, and a loose-channel guard is next. A synthetic subject that emits the right SSVEP for the stimulus log exercises the whole stack without a person.

\section{Results}

All results are from one subject, the first author, September to October 2026.

\textbf{Where the eye answers.} The first sweep (Fig.~\ref{fig:sweep}) put the response between 7.5 and 12\,Hz: Oz line ratios of 9.0 at 10.4\,Hz and 5.9 at 11.4\,Hz against 1.0 with the screen off, nothing at 20\,Hz. Mann's 15\,Hz sits on the weak side of this curve, so our sessions use 12\,Hz, the nearest frequency the display delivers exactly. A stock Muse without the cup showed no response. Measure the subject first.

\textbf{A legible word.} ``NO'' in a pixel font on 9 by 5 positions (Fig.~\ref{fig:no}): $r = 0.79$ after one pass, 0.88 after two, 0.91 after three, nulls at most 0.23, 43 of 45 positions right after the threshold, 18 minutes. A square 4.6 times larger reached 0.87 in two passes, so a larger square did not buy signal; a scalable font squeezed onto 48 positions was a blob before any brain was involved ($r = 0.41$ to 0.49 against nulls to 0.48).

\textbf{Eight colours over three sittings.} Table~\ref{tab:colour} and Fig.~\ref{fig:colour9} and~\ref{fig:planes}. The first sitting read red 32, green 28 and blue 28 of 32, giving 24 of 32 cells in the right colour and the hue right on 19 of 24 coloured cells, $r = 0.79$ against nulls of 0.32. Two hours later, 20 of 32: green's second pass helped, blue's second pass was lost to the right ear electrode coming loose at minute 11. Two days later, 23 of 32, red perfect again, blue lost on the right half of the field with every electrode holding. One wrong cell in four on the chart is almost entirely blue's.

\textbf{A sealed acuity read-out.} A tumbling E and a ring that is closed (O) or missing its bottom (U) sit on a 12 by 7 grid; one of the eight combinations is drawn at random and sealed before the scan. A template correlation on the EEG picture names the orientation and the ring, then the seal is opened. On the first human run (Fig.~\ref{fig:acuity}) the picture reached $r = 0.89$, 80 of 84 cells, and the read-out said E up, ring U, with margins of 0.24 and 0.22 over the runners-up; the seal agreed. One trial at a one-in-eight chance is a demonstration, not an acuity measurement; a series over optotype sizes is the next step \cite{norcia1985sweep}.

\textbf{Toward a real photograph.} The 2019 portrait went in as two tones and came back a silhouette. We prepare the same at our grid with a forced choice: four portraits (Fig.~\ref{fig:gallery}) cut to 16 by 16 cells at four grey levels, the block-portrait size at which a face is recognisable \cite{harmon1973masking, harmon1973faces}; one is drawn and sealed, and the read-out names the person by correlation against all four. On the clean targets the choice is right 100\% of the time with a quarter of the cells flipped, chance one in four. A colour photograph does not survive the three planes (its colours sit mid-cube and flip), but a flat illustration in the eight corner colours does (Fig.~\ref{fig:gallery}, right). \todo{wedge result, first sealed photograph, officer scan}

\textbf{A three-dimensional object.} The planes scan reads any colour image at grid size, so an object's texture atlas can go through it and come back painted on the object. A die's six faces were unfolded into a 54-cell atlas of black, white and one red pip, and scanned at 5, 5 and 8\,s per cell in one pass with the ten least certain blue cells shown again, 18 minutes in a rested morning sitting. Snapped to the die's three colours, 51 of 54 cells came back right (black 19 of 20, white 31 of 33, the pip in place) and the cube is painted from the EEG (Fig.~\ref{fig:die}); on the stricter rule used for the chart, right in all three planes at once, 35 of 54, with per-plane $r$ of 0.66, 0.67 and 0.70 against a time-shifted null of 0.12. The misses are two white cells read as pips and one black cell read white. \todo{credit the mesh3d author among the co-authors}

\textbf{Eye strain from the same recording.} After two colour sittings in one evening the subject reported irritated eyes, a slight forehead ache and no drowsiness. On a Muse under a headset the blink is best seen through the Fpz reference on the far electrodes, as a shared deflection on TP9, TP10 and the cup. Counted so, the blink rate was 6.8 per minute in the first sitting and 12.1 in the second against 3 to 4 on a reference day, with amplitude and duration unchanged. Within a sitting (Fig.~\ref{fig:blink}) blinks nearly stop while a cell flickers and burst within a second of it going dark, 50 to 65 per minute in that second, a suppress-and-release pattern the reference day lacks \cite{stern1994blink, sheedy2003cvs}. Drowsiness markers were flat: no rise in frontal theta, no long blinks; a rise in alpha seen earlier was the loose ear electrode. The forehead ache is not in the EEG (frontalis 1.5 to 2.3\,$\mu$V rms); the headset's facial interface is the likelier cause. The SSVEP itself fell by half between the blue planes of the two sittings (Fig.~\ref{fig:fatigue}), and the cup's noise rose from minute 20 of the second, so part of what reads as fatigue is contact.

\textbf{The ear as a microphone.} The same headband reads sound as well as sight. The first two paragraphs of \emph{Alice's Adventures in Wonderland} (31.5\,s of a public-domain LibriVox reading) were played 24 times from the headset's own speakers while the subject sat still with the eyes on a dot. The brain follows the loudness of speech, and a linear decoder can read that envelope back from scalp EEG \cite{osullivan2015cocktail, accou2023vlaai}. Ours is a ridge decoder on the five channels at lags from $-125$ to $+500$\,ms, reading the 1 to 8\,Hz envelope; every play is decoded by a model trained on the other plays, the first and last 3\,s of each play are left out so onset and offset responses do not count, and the mean of the held-out reconstructions is the brain's envelope of the clip (Fig.~\ref{fig:ear}). On a morning sitting with every electrode in contact it correlated with the true envelope at $r = 0.36$ over 23 plays (circular-shift null 95\% 0.10, $p < 0.001$), one play at 0.086 (null 0.022), and the same decoder with the EEG shifted off the clips at chance (0.035, $p = 0.29$); the night before, with the cup not in contact, 0.18. The signal is on the ear electrodes and the cup (TP10 0.20, AF8 0.16, Oz 0.15, TP9 0.14 alone), AF7 nothing. Research caps with 64 channels reach 0.10 to 0.19 per play \cite{accou2023vlaai}; what the headband lacks in channels it recovers by repetition. The reconstruction is written back as sound, the envelope on white noise and on the clip's own fine structure. The headset's speakers sit beside TP9 and TP10, so part of the response could be sound induced into the electrodes; the control with the headband on the desk and nobody wearing it, and a sitting with speakers across the desk, are pending. \todo{desk control; PC-speaker sitting}

\section{Toward a Screening Tool for the Field}

What a community health worker would do with this decides the design. The metaveillography lineage names the readings: a visual field, an acuity and an attention state, the last from how the field narrows and widens \cite{mann2019eye, garcia2020painting, garcia2021acuity}. Three readings come from one sitting under half an hour. A \emph{colour response profile}: the per-colour calibrations are three numbers before any scan, the raw material of an objective colour-vision screen once the stimuli are isoluminant. \rf{what a colour screen must report for a worker to act on it} An \emph{acuity read-out}: the sealed optotype, then a threshold series, giving acuity without a verbal response, as the sweep VEP does in a clinic \cite{norcia1985sweep}. An \emph{eye-strain reading and a prompt}: the blink pattern and rate are live from the same headband, and a ``rest your eyes'' message is the simplest just-in-time intervention \cite{nahum2018jitai}. \rf{prompt design and what to log around it}

\textbf{Limits.} One subject. One cell in four wrong on a 32-cell colour chart; a 96-cell picture is an hour. Blue is fragile, and 9\,Hz sits next to alpha. Contact is the largest practical risk: the cup's $\dprime$ ranged from 0.8 to 5.6 across days, and one loose electrode cost a plane. Flicker at 3 to 30\,Hz can provoke seizures in photosensitive epilepsy and the method must not be used with anyone with that history. The eye-strain result is one evening without a control, and the ear result is two sittings with the sound from the headset's own speakers. \rf{field constraints: time per subject, lighting, who holds the laptop} Next: two more subjects, the acuity series, the loose-channel guard, the sealed photograph, a globe and a torus of 96 cells, the ear's desk control, the prompt.

\section{Conclusion}

The eye as a camera works on a \$250 headband with one added cup and a \$300 headset: a legible word, eight colours over three sittings, and an optotype named from the EEG before its seal was opened, from a subject who only looked at a dot. The same headband is an ear as a microphone: the loudness of a spoken passage read back from the EEG at $r = 0.36$ and played as sound. The recording that gives the image also gives the eye strain. That is the start of a visual screener in the low-cost, objective, worker-operated tradition; the rest is subjects, stimuli that separate colour from brightness, and a prompt that closes the loop.
