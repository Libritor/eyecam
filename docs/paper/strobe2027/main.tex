\title{State of Strobe: What a Consumer EEG Headband Sees of Drowsiness, Eye Strain and Fatigue Under Flicker in a VR Headset}

\author{
\IEEEauthorblockN{Alexander Vicol and Steve Mann}
\IEEEauthorblockA{University of Toronto and MannLab Canada, Toronto, Canada}
}

\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Reading an image from the eye with the steady-state visual evoked potential means minutes of 12\,Hz flicker in a headset, cell after cell, with black in between. After the first sessions the subject said the flashing seemed to make them sleepy; after a later evening of two sessions they said their eyes were irritated, their forehead ached, and they were not sleepy at all. We call the state a session leaves behind the State of Strobe and ask what a consumer headband (Muse 2 with one added occipital cup, under a Meta Quest 3S) can see of it. Twenty-three sessions over five weeks give the drowsiness answer: with the flicker line cut out of every band, theta/alpha on the occipital cup is the same while a cell flickers as while a black cell is shown seconds away (+0.001 log10, 95\% interval $-0.040$ to $+0.041$), the only drift through a session is the wet electrode settling, a fourteen-minute session with no flicker at all drifts toward the drowsy side at least as fast, and eye closure is lower under flicker, not higher. Seven sittings analysed in depth, two in one evening, three in daytime and one at half past midnight against a reference night, give the rest: the blink rate on task doubled from the first sitting of the evening to the second (7.8 to 12.0 per minute), sat at 4 to 6 in daytime and at 8.8 after midnight; blinks stop while a cell flickers and burst the moment it goes dark, most in the evening; the forehead ache is in neither the muscle nor the EEG; and the SSVEP itself faded through the second evening sitting, collapsed within two minutes after midnight on a clean electrode, and held through two morning sittings. An eye test on the same rig, which flickers one eye at a time, splits the state in two: one eye's flicker holds the blinks back as both eyes' does, a second sitting raises the rate on the same eye (5.9 to 15.4 per minute), drowsiness is absent either way, and only the SSVEP stays with its eye. Flicker in a headset is not sedating on these markers. What it costs is the eyes, and the same headband can tell when.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

The eye-as-camera method \cite{mann2019eye} reads a picture from the SSVEP \cite{norcia2015ssvep}: a square flickers at one gaze position after another, and the response strength at each is the pixel. Our consumer version runs the stimulus in a Quest 3S browser and records with a Muse 2 headband plus one cup electrode at Oz (Fig.~
ef{fig:rig}). A session is twenty to thirty-five minutes of 12\,Hz flicker (9\,Hz for the blue plane) interleaved with black cells, in a dark headset, with the gaze held on a dot.

The name follows our State-of-Float study, also a consumer headband reading the state an experience leaves behind \cite{mann2026float}. Two reports from the subject motivated this paper. After the early sessions, that the flashing seemed to induce drowsiness and help with sleep. After two colour sessions in one evening, that the eyes were irritated and the forehead ached, with no drowsiness. Both are about the state the strobe leaves behind, which matters for anyone who would use the method as a screening tool with a community health worker holding the laptop \cite{fletcher2010wearable, fletcher2014mental}, and for the wearable-as-window-on-state tradition that reads the body's signals in daily life and acts on them \cite{picard1997wearables, fletcher2010icalm, hernandez2013insideout, nahum2018jitai}. Flicker in the 3 to 30\,Hz band is also the stimulus that provokes photosensitive seizures \cite{fisher2005photic}, so what else it does to a person is not an idle question.

Every session we had recorded was an unplanned experiment on this: flicker and black interleaved within minutes, same electrodes, same subject. We ask the recordings three things. Is the EEG drowsier under flicker than under black seconds away, and does any drowsiness marker climb with minutes on task? What do the eyes do, blink by blink? And does the SSVEP itself hold up through a sitting? The answers come from twenty-three sessions for the drowsiness question and from seven sittings in depth for the rest, including two daytime scans, a sitting at half past midnight, a morning scan of a three-dimensional object, and one session of the same length with sound and no flicker at all. A fourth question comes from an eye test built on the same rig, which flickers one eye at a time: which parts of the state belong to an eye and which to the person? One subject throughout.

\section{Data and Methods}

\textbf{Sessions.} Twenty-three sessions between September 14 and October 17, 2026, with an Oz channel and a known delivered flicker frequency, 3 to 35 minutes of stimulation each, all at night except four. Seven are analysed in depth (Table~\ref{tab:sittings}): a reference night with no symptom report, the two colour sittings of October 10 after which the eyes and forehead were reported, a grey acuity scan at noon on October 11, a colour sitting at 10:40 on October 12, a grey portrait scan begun at 00:35 on October 17 and stopped after 48 cells, and a colour scan of a die's unfolded faces at 10:45 the same morning. One more recording is the flicker-free control the within-session contrasts lack: fourteen minutes of recorded speech played from the headset at 01:35 on October 17 with the eyes open on a dot and nothing flickering, same headband, same headset.

\textbf{Markers.} Per 2\,s epoch after a 1 to 40\,Hz band-pass, with the flicker line, its harmonics and its subharmonic cut out of every band (12\,Hz and its 6\,Hz subharmonic sit inside alpha and theta and would otherwise read as drowsiness): log10 theta/alpha (4 to 7.5 over 8 to 10.5\,Hz), log10 (theta + alpha)/beta and log10 alpha power on Oz and on the ears; eye closure as the share of time with Oz alpha above three times the eyes-open baseline for a second or more. Epochs over 150\,$\mu$V or more than 20\% blink are left out. Two contrasts: \emph{acute}, each flicker epoch against the nearest black epoch within 45\,s, so time on task is matched; and \emph{drift}, the Theil-Sen slope per 10 minutes over flicker epochs and over black epochs separately, so a sedating flicker would show as the first exceeding the second.

\textbf{Blinks.} Every Muse channel is referenced at Fpz, the most blink-sensitive spot on the head, so a blink appears as one identical negative pulse of about 60\,$\mu$V on the channels far from the eyes (TP9, TP10 and the Oz cup) and only as a 3 to 16\,$\mu$V gradient on AF7 and AF8. A frontal detector counts nothing under the headset; a far-channel detector on a 0.5 to 8\,Hz band, built independently twice, agrees to the decimal. We report the blink rate on the 12\,Hz stages and in 5-minute windows, amplitude, duration, long blinks (over 400\,ms, the sleepiness sign), and a peri-event histogram around the moment a flickering cell turns black; intervals are block bootstraps over 30\,s blocks.

\textbf{Forehead.} Frontalis EMG as 20 to 45\,Hz and 65 to 120\,Hz power on AF7 and AF8 with every stimulus line and the mains cut out, its ratio to the ear and Oz channels, bursts above three times the session median, and the 60\,Hz pick-up as the contact proxy.

\textbf{The SSVEP as its own fatigue meter.} For the colour sittings, every lit cell's response (power at the flicker frequency and its harmonic over the 14 to 50\,Hz floor, the driver's own pixel value) is plotted against minutes on task next to the Oz cup's broadband noise and mains pick-up per minute, so a fading response can be told from a drying contact.

\textbf{Confounds.} Contact, whole-head movement, leakage of the stimulus line into the forehead channels, jaw muscle on the ears and the missing eyes-open baseline were each measured before any marker was read, and every October 10 finding was recomputed by two independent analyses briefed to refute it; nineteen checks completed and agreed.

\section{Results}

\textbf{Drowsiness: no, on 23 sessions.} Fig.~\ref{fig:effects} pools the sessions. Acutely, with flicker on screen the EEG is not drowsier than with black on screen seconds away: Oz theta/alpha in the calibration blocks +0.009 (95\% interval $-0.053$ to $+0.071$, 9 of 19 sessions positive), in the scans +0.001 ($-0.040$ to $+0.041$); (theta + alpha)/beta and alpha power are flat as well. The one contrast below $p = 0.05$ goes the other way: on the ears, (theta + alpha)/beta is lower on the lit cell ($-0.042$, $p = 0.023$), a bright flashing field being mildly alerting, not sleep. The drift through a session is real but not from the flicker: Oz theta/alpha falls $-0.29$ log10 per 10 minutes over flicker epochs and $-0.32$ over black epochs, the same slope either way, absent on the ears, and far too large for a brain state. It is the wet cup settling, less low-frequency noise reading as less theta (Fig.~\ref{fig:courses}). Eye closure is lower under flicker than under black ($-0.021$, $p = 0.030$). The daytime sittings add nothing in either direction: the grey scan drifts like the rest ($-0.21$ and $-0.16$), the morning colour scan sits at $+0.06$ and $+0.05$, and the die scan at $-0.20$ and $-0.12$, each the same over flicker and dark. The flicker-free session settles it from the other side (Fig.~\ref{fig:flickerfree}): with speech instead of flicker, at 01:35, Oz theta/alpha was flat ($+0.01$ per 10 minutes, $-0.09$ to $+0.11$) and the ears' (theta + alpha)/beta rose $+0.16$ per 10 minutes ($+0.06$ to $+0.25$), more than the $+0.07$ it rises over flicker epochs in the 23 flicker sessions. Whatever drift there is toward the drowsy side belongs to the hour and the minutes seated, not to the strobe.

\textbf{The eyes: strained by the second sitting of an evening.} Fig.~\ref{fig:blinks} and Table~\ref{tab:sittings}. On October 10 the blink rate on task was 7.8 per minute in the first sitting and 12.0 in the second, against 4.1 on the reference night, and the second sitting ran at twice the first at matched minutes (+6.1 per minute, 95\% interval +4.3 to +7.9, permutation $p < 0.001$) from its first five minutes (14.0 against 4.5), with the rate flat within each sitting. Blink amplitude and duration were unchanged, so the eye blinked more, not differently: the signature of a drying, irritated cornea \cite{stern1994blink, sheedy2003cvs}. The two daytime sittings, each the first of its day, sat at 4.7 and 5.8 per minute, with the reference night and with each other, and both rose by about half from their first half to their second (3.7 to 5.7 and 3.7 to 5.8), a within-sitting climb that neither evening sitting showed. The evening doubling is therefore not what a sitting does to the eyes by itself. It is what a second sitting does after a first one and the hour between. The two October 17 sittings fall where the hour predicts: the grey scan begun at 00:35, the first sitting of its night, ran at 8.8 per minute, with the first evening sitting of October 10; the die scan at 10:45, rested, at 5.5, with the daytime ones.

\textbf{Blinks held back under flicker, released on black.} While a 12\,Hz cell flickered the subject nearly stopped blinking (2 to 5 per minute) and let a burst go the moment it went dark: 27 and 36 blinks per minute in the first second after the cell turned black on the two evening sittings, against 10 and 19 in the following three seconds (Fig.~\ref{fig:release}). The reference night showed the same shape at a lower level (8 against 3), the daytime grey scan a clear one (18 against 4), and the morning colour scan almost none (8 against 6). The right panel of Fig.~\ref{fig:release} gives the same thing as rates on lit cells, black cells and the rest breaks, where the rate reaches 20 to 35 per minute. Suppress-then-release is eyes held open on a bright target, which is how a cornea dries, and it was strongest where the blink rate was highest. No long blinks appeared at any point (0 to 1\% over 400\,ms), the sleepiness sign.

\textbf{The forehead: not in the muscle, not in the EEG.} Frontalis power sat at 1.5 to 2.3\,$\mu$V rms in all three October sittings analysed for it, 10 to 100 times below a sustained contraction, with the sitting before the report at the lowest level and falling (Fig.~\ref{fig:forehead}). One episode, the left forehead electrode's broadband power tripling for the last three minutes of the first sitting, is undecidable between muscle and a shifting pad. Frontal theta did not rise with the flicker ($-5$ to $-13$\%), did not climb within any sitting, and matched the reference night. A headache has no EEG signature and the forehead electrodes show nothing that would accompany one; the headset's facial interface, which the EEG cannot see, is the likelier cause. An earlier reading of rising ear alpha in the second sitting was the right ear electrode coming loose at minute 11 and is retracted.

\section{One Eye or Two}

\textbf{What belongs to an eye and what the two share.} Every scan above is binocular: the stimulus is a 2-D page in the headset's browser and reaches both eyes. The eye test we built on the same rig flickers one eye at a time while the subject covers the other, and that splits the State of Strobe in two. The SSVEP starts in one retina and stays that eye's until the primary visual cortex, so its strength and its fade can differ between the eyes. The blink is one command to both lids, and drowsiness belongs to the whole brain. If the strain is carried by the blink, one eye's flicker should hold both eyes open, and the drowsiness answer should not depend on how many eyes see the flicker.

\textbf{Data.} Three sittings of the eye test with one eye covered, October 28 at 18:12 (right eye, then the left at 18:29) and October 29 at 15:50 and 16:21 (the right eye both times), and one with both eyes open, October 27 at 00:40, give five segments of 7 to 16 minutes. A block is 4\,s of 12\,Hz flicker (the whole window, or a pattern reversing at 12\,Hz) and 4\,s off, black after the flash and mean grey after a pattern. The detector and the markers are the ones above; the flicker-rate sweep, whose 6 to 10\,Hz rates fall in theta and alpha, is left out of the EEG contrast.

\textbf{Blinks held back under one eye's flicker.} With one eye covered the subject blinked 2.6 to 10.2 times per minute while the flicker was on and 5.6 to 16.5 while it was off, 1.6 to 2.8 times as often off as on (Fig.~\ref{fig:oneeye}). With both eyes open the same test gave 5.4 against 11.3, and the binocular scans 2.1 to 5.9 against 4.4 to 16.4. The difference is positive in all five segments and its interval clears zero in three. The lids move together, so a strobe in one eye holds both open: covering an eye does not spare it the held-back blinks, only closing it does. What the test's regular 4\,s blocks did not show, with one eye or two, is the release: the first second after the flicker stopped was not clearly above the next three in any segment (8/4, 12/12, 6/7 and 10/16 per minute with one eye, 15/10 with two, every interval spanning zero).

\textbf{The second sitting again, with the eye held fixed.} On October 29 the right eye was tested at 15:50 and again at 16:21. The blink rate went from 5.9 per minute (3.4 to 9.0) to 15.4 (11.7 to 18.8), the same second-sitting rise as October 10, here on one eye with the other covered both times. On October 28 the left eye, tested 17 minutes after the right and 70 minutes into the recording, blinked 8.8 times per minute against 4.6. The test always takes the right eye first, so that difference belongs to the order as much as to the eye.

\textbf{Drowsiness: no, one eye or two.} Oz theta/alpha, flicker epoch minus the nearest off epoch, was $+0.006$, $+0.001$, $+0.089$ and $-0.029$ log10 in the four one-eye segments (57 to 172 pairs each) and $+0.063$ with both eyes, inside the spread of the binocular sessions, and eye closure stayed under 0.09 of the time. The ears' (theta + alpha)/beta was lower under flicker in all five segments ($-0.004$ to $-0.102$), the alerting direction the 23 scans showed.

\textbf{The SSVEP stays with its eye.} The flash, five 4\,s blocks of full-window 12\,Hz flicker against five of black, gave each eye its own response on the Oz cup: 3.6\,$\mu$V for the right eye and 5.9\,$\mu$V for the left on October 28 ($p = 0.024$ and $0.008$), and for the right eye 3.0\,$\mu$V in the first sitting of October 29 and 2.2\,$\mu$V in the second ($p = 0.004$ and $0.016$). The second sitting's response is a quarter lower, the camera's fatigue meter again, though five blocks per sitting cannot separate that from the block-to-block spread (sd 1.2 and 0.7\,$\mu$V).

\textbf{A sixth day.} On November 3 the eye test gave five more segments: the left eye at 11:48 and 12:08, the right at 12:21, and each eye at 14:15 and 14:21 through a light ladder that halves the flash from full white down to 0.8\% in eight steps (a sitting at 11:42 lost the Oz cup and is left out). In all five the subject blinked less under the flicker than off it, 0.4 to 3.9 against 5.8 to 9.3 per minute, and the ladder, most of whose levels are far dimmer than a scan, held the blinks back the most (0.4 and 1.2 against 6.0 and 9.3): dim flicker holds the lids too. Oz theta/alpha, flicker minus off, stayed between $-0.071$ and $+0.069$ log10, no drowsier on the sixth day than on the first. With the left eye first this time, the second eye blinked less (3.6 against 5.8 per minute), not more as on October 28, so order alone does not explain that difference, though the intervals overlap. The flash came back in every segment, 4.0 to 5.1\,$\mu$V in the morning, but at 2.3\,$\mu$V in the right eye's second sitting, two hours after its 5.1: the per-eye fade again. The same day's rate sweep put the left eye's best response at 12\,Hz, half gone by 23.6\,Hz, the rate the scans use.

\textbf{What one eye buys.} Three things follow. The strain reading is the person's, not the eye's: the far-channel blink cannot tell which eye the strobe was in, and a second sitting raised it on a covered-eye test as it did on a binocular scan. Safety comes cheap: closing or covering one eye is the standing advice to people with photosensitive epilepsy facing a flickering source, since it halves the stimulus \cite{tychsen2020vr}, and a headset can do it in software by flickering one display and leaving the other dark. And the SSVEP can carry a fatigue meter per eye at no extra cost: give each eye its own rate, 12\,Hz to one and 15\,Hz to the other, and one Oz cup reads both responses at once \cite{brown1997rivalry}, with their binocular interaction at the sum frequency \cite{baker2017optimal}.

\section{What It Means}

\textbf{For the subject.} Flicker at 12\,Hz in a headset, in sittings of twenty to thirty-five minutes, did not make this subject drowsy on any marker the headband can measure: not acutely, not over a sitting, not on twenty-one sessions. What it did was dry the eyes, and the headband saw it two ways. The report of sleepiness after the early sessions is not contradicted by this; it is simply not in the EEG, and several of those sessions were recorded around two in the morning, when the hour explains more than the screen. The published after-effect of alpha-band flicker is a drop in alpha power, not a slide toward theta \cite{herrmann2001flicker}, consistent with what we see and not a sleep-onset signature.

\textbf{For the camera.} One sitting per evening: the second began with the eyes blinking twice as fast and read 20 of 32 colour cells where the first read 24. A pass is only worth averaging in when the electrodes held through it, so a loose-channel guard belongs in the decoder. And rest breaks are where the eyes recover, at five to ten times the rate on task, so a break every forty cells is part of the measurement.

\textbf{For a screener.} The same recording that gives the picture gives the eye-strain reading, live, from the far channels, with no extra sensor. A ``rest your eyes'' prompt on the page when the release pattern sharpens or the rate crosses a threshold is the simplest just-in-time intervention \cite{nahum2018jitai} and the first the system should carry, in the tradition of sensor-plus-app systems that read a state and answer it \cite{fletcher2011cbt, boyer2012iheal, leonard2018homeless}.

\textbf{Limits.} One subject, one headband, one headset. Drowsiness after a session, rather than during it, is invisible to a within-session contrast. There is no sleepiness rating and no reaction-time test, and the one flicker-free control is a single night session with sound in place of flicker rather than silence, so the eye-strain result is a between-sitting difference with the time of evening confounded. In the eye test the subject covered the other eye, so whether it was open behind the cover is not recorded, and the right eye went first on every day but one. Flicker in this band must never be used with anyone with a history of photosensitive epilepsy \cite{fisher2005photic}.

\section{A Study That Could Answer It}

The stimulus page already does everything needed; what is missing is the design and two questions to the subject. Five-minute blocks, counterbalanced and at matched luminance: full-field 12\,Hz flicker, static grey at the same mean luminance, and black with the dot; a sleepiness rating before and after every block \cite{akerstedt1990kss} and a two-minute reaction-time test after each \cite{dinges1985pvt}, both on the page; the Oz cup given five minutes to settle first; a block at the subject's own alpha frequency and one at 40\,Hz; one-eye blocks for each eye in counterbalanced order against two-eye blocks at the same luminance, the eyes tagged at their own rates once the page runs in WebXR; daytime as well as night; more than one person.

\section{Conclusion}

On every drowsiness marker a consumer headband can measure under a VR headset, 12\,Hz flicker interleaved with black is not sedating, acutely or over a sitting, on twenty-three sessions of one subject, and a session of the same length with no flicker drifts toward the drowsy side at least as fast. The state the strobe leaves behind is in the eyes: a blink rate that doubles on the second sitting of an evening and blinks held back under the flicker and released on black, with the blink itself unchanged and no sign of sleep. With one eye covered the blinks are held back just the same and the second sitting raises them on the same eye: the strain belongs to the person, while the SSVEP belongs to the eye and can be read per eye. For the camera that means one sitting per evening, rest breaks as part of the measurement and a contact guard; for a screener it means the eye-strain reading comes free with the picture, and the prompt to rest the eyes can come from the same headband.
